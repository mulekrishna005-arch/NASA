NASA SCIENTIFIC BRIEFING

Methodology, Uncertainty & Data Definitions

1. Active Fire Detection vs. Confirmed Wildfire Ground Truth

A common point of confusion is treating a satellite "hotspot" as a confirmed wildfire perimeter or burned acreage. A satellite detection is a thermal anomaly: one 375m (VIIRS) or 1km (MODIS) pixel where mid-infrared radiation exceeds background threshold. A single 5,000-acre wildfire can trigger 60 pixels, while an industrial flare or prescribed agricultural burn triggers 1 pixel. Our model forecasts satellite observation pixel counts, not wildfire perimeters.

2. Cloud & Smoke Obscuration (The "Invisible Fire" Phenomenon)

Thermal sensors operate in the 3.9 μm and 11 μm bands. Clouds and dense pyrocumulus smoke absorb and scatter thermal radiation. When heavy cloud cover occurs, observed hotspot counts can abruptly drop to zero even if smoldering continues under canopy. To account for this, the model incorporates the MODIS/VIIRS Cloud Fraction as a feature and adjusts forecast confidence accordingly.

3. Physics-Informed Gradient Boosting Engine

Forecasting next-day active-fire activity involves non-linear interactions across:

  • Dead Fuel Moisture (FDFM %): Exponential ignition probability threshold below 6.5%.
  • Atmospheric Vapour Pressure Deficit: Calculated from 2m Temperature and Relative Humidity.
  • SRTM Topographic Slope: Chimney convective acceleration where fire spreads up to 4× faster uphill.
  • Barometric Oxygen Partial Pressure (pO₂): Derived from altitude via SRTM Digital Elevation Model.
